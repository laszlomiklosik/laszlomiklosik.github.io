Types of Acoustic Guitars

The acoustic guitar family is remarkably diverse — each type shaped by tradition, culture, and sonic purpose. Here are the most iconic varieties from around the world.

Classical

Classical / Nylon String

The classical guitar, with its wide neck and nylon strings, is the direct descendant of the 19th-century Spanish guitar. Its warm, round tone makes it ideal for classical repertoire, flamenco, and fingerstyle playing. Masters like Andrés Segovia helped elevate it to concert-hall prestige.

Steel String

Steel String Acoustic

The backbone of folk, country, blues, and rock, the steel-string acoustic offers a bright, projecting tone that fills a room. Pioneered by American makers like C.F. Martin in the early 20th century, it now comes in dozens of body shapes and wood combinations.

12-String

12-String

With six paired courses tuned in octaves or unisons, the 12-string guitar produces a rich, shimmering chorus effect. Artists like Leo Kottke, Lead Belly, and Roger McGuinn of The Byrds made it famous, using its lush, full sound in folk, blues, and jangle-pop.

Baritone

Baritone

Tuned a fourth or fifth lower than standard (typically B to B or A to A), the baritone guitar has a longer scale length and a deep, resonant voice. It occupies the sonic space between a guitar and a bass, beloved by surf, alternative, and cinematic composers. Artists like Nick Cave and Tim Hecker favour its haunting low register.

Portuguese

Guitarra Portuguesa

The soul of Fado, the Portuguese guitar (Guitarra Portuguesa) is a pear-shaped, 12-string instrument with metal strings and a distinctive fan-shaped headstock. Its bright, metallic timbre and ornate decoration make it one of the most visually and sonically distinctive guitars in the world. Coimbra and Lisbon styles differ subtly in tuning and ornamentation.

Resonator

Resonator

Invented in the 1920s to project louder sound before amplification, the resonator guitar uses a metal cone inside the body to amplify the strings. Its distinctive, raw twang became central to Delta blues, slide guitar, and bluegrass. Dobro and National are the iconic brands associated with this powerful design.

Travel

Travel Guitar

Compact and lightweight, travel guitars are built for portability without fully sacrificing playability. Brands like Martin Backpacker, Taylor GS Mini, and Baby Taylor make excellent instruments for musicians on the move. Despite their small stature, many offer surprisingly full tone.

Archtop

Archtop

With a carved or pressed arched top and f-holes like a cello, the archtop guitar blends the worlds of jazz and classical lutherie. Orville Gibson pioneered the design; later makers like D'Angelico and Benedetto refined it to perfection. Its warm, round projection and sustain make it the jazz guitarist's instrument of choice.

History of the Guitar

From ancient plucked lutes to the modern concert hall and recording studio, the guitar has a lineage stretching back more than 3,500 years. Each era added new forms, tunings, and techniques that shaped the instrument we know today.

c. 3500 BC

Ancient Origins

Long-necked lutes first appear in Mesopotamia (modern Iraq), depicted on seals and pottery. These forerunners — played by pressing strings along a neck to change pitch — established the essential guitar concept. Similar instruments spread through Egypt and Persia, evolving into the oud, which remains central to Middle Eastern and North African music.

13th–15th Century

The Medieval Gittern & Lute

The gittern — a small, gut-strung lute — was popular in medieval Europe, played at court and in taverns. Alongside it, the oud evolved into the European lute, which became the dominant plucked instrument of the Renaissance. The Moorish and Latin guitars of Spain began diverging: the Moorish guitar had a rounded back; the Latin guitar was flat-backed and more closely resembled today's instrument.

16th Century

The Renaissance Vihuela

In Spain and Italy, the vihuela — a guitar-shaped instrument with lute-like tuning — became fashionable among the nobility. Composers like Luis de Milán wrote virtuosic fantasias for it. The four-course guitar (with four pairs of strings) also emerged and quickly spread through Europe as a more accessible, popular alternative to the lute.

17th Century

Baroque Guitar

The five-course Baroque guitar replaced the four-course instrument and became the fashionable instrument of 17th-century Europe. Composers Francesco Corbetta and Robert de Visée wrote sophisticated music for it, and it was played at the courts of Louis XIV and Charles II. Its strumming style and ornate decoration reflected Baroque aesthetics.

Early 19th Century

The Classical Guitar is Born

Spanish luthier Antonio de Torres Jurado revolutionised guitar construction in the 1850s–1860s by enlarging the body, developing the fan-bracing system, and standardising the modern classical guitar. His designs established the template still used today. Fernando Sor, Mauro Giuliani, and Dionisio Aguado composed the canonical classical repertoire and elevated the guitar to a serious concert instrument.

1833 – Early 20th Century

C.F. Martin & the American Steel-String

German immigrant Christian Frederick Martin founded C.F. Martin & Co. in 1833, eventually settling in Nazareth, Pennsylvania. Martin pioneered X-bracing — a pattern of internal struts that could withstand the higher tension of steel strings — and developed the iconic Dreadnought body shape (with John Thomas Doyle) in 1916. Gibson, founded in 1902 by Orville Gibson, introduced the archtop and the round-shouldered Jumbo, competing fiercely and expanding the acoustic guitar's sonic palette.

1920s–1940s

Blues, Folk & Country

The acoustic guitar became the voice of American roots music. Delta blues artists like Robert Johnson and Charley Patton defined a raw, soulful style. The Carter Family popularised it in country music, while Woody Guthrie made it a symbol of protest and folk storytelling. The resonator guitar (National, Dobro) was invented in 1927 to project louder sound before amplification was widely available.

1960s–Present

The Folk Revival, Singer-Songwriters & Beyond

The 1960s folk revival — Bob Dylan, Joan Baez, Joni Mitchell — made the acoustic guitar the emblem of a generation. Taylor Guitars was founded in 1974, bringing modern manufacturing precision and innovative designs like the Grand Auditorium. Today, luthiers worldwide experiment with non-traditional tonewoods, carbon fibre, and electronics, while the acoustic guitar remains at the heart of countless musical traditions globally.

Alternate Tunings

Standard tuning (E A D G B E) is just the starting point. Alternate tunings open up entirely new voicings, resonances, and sonic landscapes — many rooted in centuries-old traditions.

Open G
D G D G B D

Beloved by blues and rock slide players. The strings form a G major chord open. Keith Richards famously removed the low E string and played Open G for much of the Rolling Stones' catalogue. Robert Johnson also used this tuning.

♪ Brown Sugar, Little Red Rooster
Open D
D A D F♯ A D

Another classic slide and bottleneck tuning, forming a D major chord open. Richie Havens used it for his powerful strummed style — including the iconic Woodstock performance of "Freedom." Joni Mitchell also explored it extensively.

♪ Freedom (Havens), Slide Blues
DADGAD
D A D G A D

Discovered by Davy Graham after encountering Moroccan musicians in Tangiers, DADGAD creates a suspended, modal sound that fits both Celtic fingerstyle and Middle Eastern music perfectly. Jimmy Page used it for "Kashmir." Pierre Bensusan is its modern virtuoso.

♪ Kashmir, She Moved Through the Fair
Drop D
D A D G B E

The most popular alternate tuning — only the low E is dropped to D. This enables powerful, open-fifth power chords on the bottom three strings and a thunderous bass note. Used extensively in folk (Neil Young), acoustic rock, and metal.

♪ Harvest Moon, Heart of Gold
Open E
E B E G♯ B E

Open E produces a bright, resonant E major chord and is a favourite of electric blues and acoustic slide players. Duane Allman and Derek Trucks used it extensively. Because it raises several strings, it places extra tension on the neck — some players tune down to Open D and use a capo.

♪ Layla (slide parts), Little Wing
Open A
E A E A C♯ E

Open A produces a bright, punchy A major chord. It's closely related to Open G (just a step up) and similarly beloved for slide and blues. Mississippi Fred McDowell and Ry Cooder have used this tuning to great effect.

♪ Shake 'Em On Down, Motherless Children
Open C
C G C G C E

A deep, sonorous tuning that produces a rich, full C major chord and enables extensive use of open strings as drones. John Butler (of the John Butler Trio) uses a variation of this tuning to spectacular effect in his fingerstyle guitar work.

♪ Ocean (John Butler), Open C pieces
Double Drop D
D A D G B D

Both E strings are dropped to D, creating a warm, folk-friendly resonance. Neil Young used Double Drop D for "Cinnamon Girl," where the two D strings create a droning, hypnotic quality. Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young also explored this tonality.

♪ Cinnamon Girl, The Needle and the Damage Done

Sizes & Body Forms

The body shape of an acoustic guitar profoundly affects its tone, projection, and playability. From intimate parlor guitars to booming jumbos, each form has its own character and best-use scenario.

♬
Parlor
Body length ~18.5"

The smallest of the traditional acoustic guitars, parlors were the dominant form in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — small enough to be played in sitting rooms (hence the name). Their compact size yields a focused, midrange-heavy tone that sits beautifully in a mix. Blues legends like Robert Johnson almost certainly played parlor guitars.

♪ Tone: Focused, midrange, intimate
♬
Concert (OO)
Body length ~19"

Slightly larger than the parlor, the Concert size (Martin's "OO" designation) offers a balanced tone with more bass response than a parlor but still manageable for smaller players. It's a favourite for fingerpickers and singer-songwriters who want clarity without overwhelming volume.

♪ Tone: Balanced, clear, articulate
♬
Auditorium (000 / OM)
Body length ~19.5"

The Auditorium (000) and Orchestra Model (OM) are among the most versatile acoustic guitars. They offer a full tonal range — present bass, clear midrange, and sparkling treble — and are comfortable for both strumming and fingerpicking. Eric Clapton's famous unplugged sessions used a Martin 000-42.

♪ Tone: Versatile, full-range, responsive
♬
Grand Auditorium
Body length ~20"

Taylor's GA (Grand Auditorium) body, introduced in 1994, quickly became one of the best-selling acoustic shapes in the world. It combines the waist curve of the OM with a broader lower bout for more volume and bass, making it ideal for a wide range of playing styles from strumming to intricate fingerstyle.

♪ Tone: Balanced, full, versatile
♬
Dreadnought
Body length ~20"

Named after the massive WWI battleship, the Dreadnought is the most iconic acoustic guitar body shape in the world. Developed by C.F. Martin around 1916, its large, square-shouldered body produces powerful bass response and high volume — perfect for flatpicking, bluegrass, and rhythm strumming. The Martin D-28 is perhaps the most copied acoustic guitar in history.

♪ Tone: Powerful, bass-rich, loud
♬
Jumbo
Body length ~20.5"

Gibson's J-200 (introduced 1938) defined the Jumbo form — a rounded, deep body with massive volume and a pronounced bass. It's the guitar of choice for big strummers and performers who need to fill a large room acoustically. Elvis Presley's iconic strumming and many country artists have favoured the Jumbo's theatrical presence.

♪ Tone: Huge, booming, warm
♬
Grand Concert (OO)
Body length ~19.25"

Taylor's Grand Concert is a smaller-bodied guitar designed for players who prefer a comfortable, intimate feel. Its tighter waist and smaller lower bout produce a focused, nuanced tone that rewards careful fingerstyle technique. Excellent for recording sessions where too much bass can cause muddiness.

♪ Tone: Detailed, articulate, recording-friendly
♬
Travel / Mini
Body length ~17–18"

Travel guitars prioritise portability. The Martin Backpacker, Taylor GS Mini, Baby Taylor, and Little Martin are popular examples. Despite their reduced size, modern travel guitars achieve surprisingly full tone — the GS Mini in particular has earned a devoted following as a primary instrument, not just a travel compromise.

♪ Tone: Compact, surprisingly full, bright
